JOURNAL 06 / MATH

Moving from Worked Examples to Independent Problems

A long-form guide to scaffolding, fading support and varied practice using clear outcomes, active practice and realistic review.

01

Define the result before the session

Begin by turning one worked solution into an outcome that can be seen, heard or checked. A vague intention such as “understand more” offers no clear finish and makes it difficult to choose a useful task. Instead, decide what you should be able to explain, solve, compare, produce or revise by the end of the block. In this guide, the focus is scaffolding, fading support and varied practice. Keep the outcome small enough for the time available and meaningful enough to reveal a change in understanding. A learner who knows the intended result can select materials more carefully, notice when the work has drifted and stop with a useful record rather than simply stopping when energy runs out.

Write the outcome as a verb plus an object and, when helpful, a condition. “Explain the stages from memory with one example” is easier to evaluate than “review the stages.” Prepare three related problems only after the outcome is clear. This order prevents resources from deciding the session for you. It also reduces the common pattern of opening many tabs, collecting several explanations and spending the available time organising rather than learning. The aim is not to make every session rigid. A clear outcome creates enough structure to make adjustment possible when the material proves easier, harder or more connected than expected.

02

Map what you know before adding more

Before rereading, write or say what you can already reconstruct about scaffolding, fading support and varied practice. Use a blank page, a short voice note or a few rough boxes and arrows. Do not wait for a polished answer. The first attempt is diagnostic: it exposes which ideas are available, which links are weak and which words feel familiar without carrying an explanation. Compare the attempt with the source only after you have made your current model visible. This sequence turns review into a response to evidence. It also prevents fluency—the ease of looking at familiar material—from being mistaken for knowledge you can use without the source present.

When you check, mark additions and corrections differently from the original attempt. That distinction preserves evidence of the gap. Look for steps chosen, hints needed and transfer to a new format. Avoid copying entire passages into the record, because a complete copy can hide the exact connection that was missing. Write the smallest correction that repairs the model: one definition, one causal link, one example or one condition. If the gap is large, reduce the scope of the session rather than racing through more material. Learning plans improve when they respond to the quality of recall, not when they defend a schedule that no longer fits the evidence.

03

Choose practice that matches the future use

Practice should require the decision you eventually need to make. If the goal is explanation, practise organising ideas and choosing examples rather than only recognising definitions. If the goal is problem solving, include questions where the method is not named in advance. If the goal is writing, produce a claim and reasoning chain instead of reviewing model paragraphs for a long time. For scaffolding, fading support and varied practice, select a short task that moves one step beyond the supported example. Keep the difficulty high enough to require attention but low enough that feedback can still guide a correction.

Use several small attempts instead of one long performance when the skill is new. After each attempt, check the process before checking the final answer. Ask which cue led to the first decision, where the connection became uncertain and whether a different representation would help. Record only the feedback you can apply in the next attempt. Too much feedback creates another reading task and can make the learner dependent on a complete solution. A useful correction points to the next action while preserving enough of the problem for the learner to think again.

04

Separate an error from a judgment

An error is information about the current strategy, not a verdict on ability. Classify what happened before choosing the response. The issue may be missing knowledge, weak recall, an incorrect method choice, incomplete instructions, rushed execution or a lapse in attention. Each cause suggests different work. Rereading can help missing knowledge but will not automatically fix a habit of choosing the first familiar method. More practice can strengthen a correct process but may also reinforce a misunderstanding if feedback arrives too late. Use the evidence from the task to choose the smallest corrective step.

Write an error note in three lines: what happened, why it probably happened and what cue should interrupt the pattern next time. Return to steps chosen, hints needed and transfer to a new format and identify one repeated signal rather than saving every small mistake. The purpose of an error log is future action, not a complete archive of imperfection. Review the note before a similar task, then remove or revise it when the pattern changes. This keeps the record light enough to use and acknowledges that learning evidence has a lifespan. Old difficulties should not become a permanent description of the learner.

05

Plan spacing around real availability

Memory benefits from returning to an idea after some forgetting, but a plan must still fit the learner’s life. Schedule the first return soon enough that the basic structure can be rebuilt, then lengthen the interval when recall becomes more reliable. Do not fill every open period with review. Protect time for new learning, varied practice and rest. For one worked solution, choose two or three meaningful return points instead of a complicated calendar that is unlikely to survive a busy week. A simple plan completed consistently produces better evidence than a perfect-looking system that is abandoned.

At each return, begin with retrieval rather than a full reread. Change the question, example or format so the idea must be used rather than merely repeated. If recall collapses, shorten the interval or reduce the amount being reviewed. If recall is strong and flexible, extend the interval and use the idea in a more demanding context. Record the adjustment beside the result. Spacing is not a fixed formula; it is a responsive decision based on the strength, accuracy and transfer of what comes back.

06

Close with evidence and a restart cue

End the session by comparing the outcome with the work produced. Note what you can now do, what remains unstable and what evidence supports that judgment. Time spent is useful context but not the main result. A twenty-minute block that produces a correct explanation and reveals one missing link may be more productive than a long session of comfortable rereading. Save the output, correction or practice result in a place you can find again. Then write the smallest next step that continues the same pathway.

Make the restart cue precise: page, problem, question, concept or first action. “Continue tomorrow” asks the next session to rediscover the work; “redo question four without the diagram, then compare” provides an immediate entry point. Review whether three related problems helped or created unnecessary friction. Remove extra resources, shorten the record and keep the parts that influenced a decision. Learn Matrix Works treats planning as a cycle: define, attempt, observe, adjust and return. The system succeeds when it makes the next useful action easier to see, not when it becomes another subject to maintain.

REUSABLE RECORD

Outcome. Evidence. Next move.

Keep the record short enough to read before the next session and specific enough to change what you do.
